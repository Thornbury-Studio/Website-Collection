December to February

Winter

Nothing is asleep. The cluster is a muscle, shivering, eating through what we left it.

There is no brood for most of it, so the colony cannot replace a single bee it loses. What is in the box in December is what has to reach March. We do not open hives; we heft them from behind, and the ones that feel light get fondant and a note in the book.

March to May

Spring

The colony is now in a race with its own mortality, and it starts the race behind.

Every bee alive in March was born the previous autumn and is running out of time. The queen has to replace them faster than they die, on willow and blackthorn alone, in weather that can shut the hedge for a fortnight. This is where the year is won: a colony that is not strong by the third week of May cannot take the flow when it comes.

June to July

The flow

A fortnight of lime, and a colony that was ready for it.

The main flow is not long. Clover carries the middle of it and lime finishes it, and between them is the June gap — a fortnight when the rape is over, the clover has not started, and a heavy colony can starve with supers on. The bees working it were laid in May. That is the whole of the argument for not letting them swarm.

August to November

Harvest

We take the crop, and then we stop thinking about honey and start thinking about March.

The balsam finishes, the crop comes off, and the only thing that matters afterwards is the September brood. Those bees never forage. They carry fat reserves, live five months instead of six weeks, and they are the colony that opens next spring. Treat late and you lose them; treat on time and you have not lost anything yet.

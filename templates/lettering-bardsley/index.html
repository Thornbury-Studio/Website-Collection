01 The hand

Turn the nib.
You are turning
the typeface.

Nothing below is an outline. Each letter is stored as the path the pen travelled — a skeleton — and the shape is worked out by sweeping a straight edge along it. The edge is held at one angle the whole way, which is why some strokes come out thick and some come out as hairlines. Move the angle and the thicks and thins move with it. Same skeleton. Different letter.

Contrast
—
  • 0° is a flat nib. Verticals come out at full width, horizontals vanish. It is the stress of a slab, and it is almost unwritable by hand.
  • 30° is where a hand actually sits. Diagonal stress, a thin on the top-left of every bowl. Every humanist face in the world is downstream of it.
  • 90° reverses the contrast: horizontals go heavy, stems go thin. Fine for a fascia, and wrong for a paragraph.

02 Releases

Four families,
one hand.

Each of these started as the same skeleton at a different tool setting, which is why the diagnostic letter beside each one is drawn by the engine above rather than set in the finished font. Licensing is per-family, perpetual, with the web licence included.

  1. Quoin

    2023 · 6 styles · display serif

    A narrow nib held close to flat, so the contrast is severe and the serifs come out as the natural end of a stroke rather than as a bracket bolted on. Cut for headlines at 40 pt and up. Below that the thins close up and it stops being readable, which is a feature of the drawing and not a fault of the printing.

  2. Gansey

    2021 · 14 styles · text family

    The workhorse. Thirty degrees, medium nib, nothing clever — a face for annual reports, timetables and the small print on the back of a ticket. Fourteen styles because a text family that cannot set a footnote and a running head from the same drawing is not finished.

  3. Mullion

    2024 · 5 styles · fascia display

    Reverse contrast, pulled off a set of pre-war shopfronts on Great Junction Street where the horizontals carry all the weight. It reads at forty metres and it is unkind at ten point. Made for boards, awnings and the top of a window.

  4. Ligger

    2025 · 2 styles · brush script

    A round nib rather than an edge, so there is no contrast at all and every join has to be earned by the drawing instead. Two weights, 380 contextual alternates, and an ampersand that does most of the work on its own.

03 On the wall

Enamel, gold,
and a steady arm.

Fascias are painted in one-shot enamel with a sable quill, freehand, off a full-size pounce pattern. Glass is gilded from the back: loose leaf floated onto gelatine size, backed up, then the letter cut through with a scalpel so the edge is glass-sharp rather than brush-sharp. Neither can be undone, which is the reason both are still worth paying for.

Lead time is six to ten weeks. I letter about twenty jobs a year and turn down rather more, usually because the sign wanted was really a vinyl cut and would be better and cheaper as one.

04 About

First floor,
above a chandler’s.

I trained as a signwriter before I ever drew a typeface, which is the wrong way round according to most people and the right way round according to me. Four years holding a quill against a wall teaches you what a stroke is for. Six years since, drawing them at 2,000 units to the em, has mostly been an argument with what the brush already knew.

The room has a north window, a plan chest of pounce patterns going back to 1961 that came with the lease, and a wall of gouache that is arranged by nothing at all. I teach a broad-nib evening class twice a year and it fills in about an hour, which says something cheerful about the state of things.

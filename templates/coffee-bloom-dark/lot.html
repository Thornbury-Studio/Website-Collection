The farm

A ridge named after a noisy bird.

Guacharacas are the chachalacas that shout from the shade trees at first light. The ridge has been called after them for longer than anyone on it can remember, and Aurora Tique’s family has farmed the top of it since 1994.

Nine hectares, almost all of it steep

Nothing on this farm is reached by vehicle. Cherry comes down the slope on people’s backs to a wet mill beside the house, and it is pulped the same afternoon it is picked.

It is all Caturra: a short tree that suits the slope, with a sweet, red-fruited cup that the altitude keeps bright. Aurora grows it under guamo and plantain for shade, which slows the cherry down and gives the sugars longer to build.

Five passes, only the red ones

A coffee tree does not ripen all at once. The pickers go through every row five times between May and July and take only the cherries that are fully red.

It is slower and costs more than stripping a branch in one go. It is also most of the reason the cup tastes of fruit rather than of paper, so it is written into what we pay: US$9.80 a kilo of green, agreed in February.

The wet mill

Pulped the same day, dried for eighteen.

From basket to tank

The cherry is floated to take off the light and damaged fruit, pulped, and left dry in a tiled tank for thirty-six hours while the mucilage breaks down.

Then it is washed in channels until the parchment squeaks. That is the actual test: someone pushes a hand through the beans and listens.

Washed, and then left alone

Washed coffees taste of the place more than of the process. That is why we buy them: one coffee all year has to be one you do not get tired of.

The parchment dries under plastic on raised beds for eighteen days, turned four times a day, until it reaches 10.5 per cent moisture.

The arithmetic

Why one lot lasts a year.

Green coffee keeps for about a year in the right room. Roasted coffee keeps for about a month. So we buy the whole harvest, keep it green, and roast only what one week will drink.

In the cup

Red grape, panela, a twist of orange.

Sweet first, like the raw cane sugar it tastes of, then red fruit, and a clean finish closer to black tea than to anything roasted.

It is at its best between day 4 and day 30. Before that it is too lively to extract evenly; after it, you can taste the flatness you saw on the right-hand dripper.

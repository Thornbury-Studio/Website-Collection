02  —  Mørketid

It isn’t one long night.

Svalbard calls this season mørketid, the dark time. In late October the sun stops clearing the mountains. By 12 November it no longer comes within six degrees of the horizon, which means even at noon there isn’t enough light outside to read by.

Then, for an hour or two around midday, the sky turns a blue you won’t have seen anywhere else. The rest belongs to the stars and the moon, and, because Svalbard sits under the daytime side of the aurora, to northern lights that can turn up at any hour. We’ve had them over breakfast.

03  —  The way in

There’s no road to us.

Fly to Longyearbyen, about three hours from Oslo. We meet the plane, fit you with a snowmobile suit, boots and mitts, and drive you out: 64 kilometres over two valleys and the tongue of a glacier, with one of our guides in front. Your bags follow on the sledge.

  2. AdventdalenThe valley out of town, under the last street lights
  3. The glacier tongueHalfway. Engines off for ten minutes to listen

Everyone who leaves a settlement in Svalbard must be protected against polar bears. Our guides carry the rifle and the flare gun so you don’t have to.

04  —  The lodge

Six rooms, one long table, a sauna on the fjord.

The house was a trapper’s station in the 1930s. We rebuilt it in black-stained larch, kept it low against the wind, and left the windows the size they were. Twelve guests at most, so the dark stays dark.

Keep going, through the window

Dinner at seven, at one table.

Arctic char, reindeer, bread from the oven by the door. Twelve chairs and one conversation, with a guide at the end who will tell you what the sky is likely to do tonight.

A north window in every room.

Four doubles and two twins, wool on the beds and no curtains on the north side. If the aurora starts at three in the morning you’ll see it from the pillow. Or ask, and we’ll knock.
